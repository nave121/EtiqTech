# Global Patterns in Ethics Request Failures (Head-to-Head Set)

This report summarizes the recurring, **essential reasons** animal‐ethics requests were not approved across all head‑to‑head cases. It is meant as a high‑level checklist for drafting stronger future protocols.

## 1. Scope, Staging, and Study Design
- **Over‑ambitious single protocols** – Several requests bundle many dependent sub‑projects (multiple models, therapies, and endpoints) into one application. Committees prefer **phased design**: first a focused feasibility/pilot to establish a model, then separate, smaller follow‑up studies.
- **Pilot vs full program mismatch** – Requests labelled “pilot” sometimes ask for 3–4 years and large N. For a true pilot, the committee expects **short duration, modest sample size, and narrow questions**.
- **Unclear feasibility logic** – When the success of a new model is uncertain (tumor model, viral construct, new imaging paradigm), that uncertainty must be contained in a **dedicated, limited‑risk protocol**, not spread across many downstream aims.

## 2. Analgesia, Anesthesia, and Euthanasia
- **Incomplete or inconsistent analgesia** – Common failures: missing pain relief for clearly painful procedures, single massive doses instead of repeated standard dosing (e.g. buprenorphine 0.05–0.1 mg/kg SC q8–12h), or contradictions between text and tables about whether analgesics are used.
- **Euthanasia not clearly defined** – The committee expects explicit, **two‑step euthanasia** for adults (e.g. CO₂ plus cervical dislocation) and age‑appropriate methods for neonates. Mixing perfusion, dislocation, and CO₂ without a clear sequence is a frequent rejection point.
- **Generic, copy‑pasted welfare text** – Re‑using euthanasia or endpoint language from unrelated models (e.g. heart‑failure endpoints in an ONC protocol) signals poor fit between welfare measures and the actual procedure.

## 3. Humane Endpoints and Monitoring
- **Endpoints not model‑specific** – Endpoints must reflect the **actual risks** of the experiment (e.g. tumor growth limits, skin‑flap necrosis, ocular complications, metabolic decompensation), not generic lists.
- **Insufficient monitoring frequency** – For high‑burden models (grade 3–5), once‑weekly checks are not acceptable. Committees expect **frequent early monitoring** (daily or every 12–48 hours post‑intervention) and clear follow‑up plans later.
- **Vague trigger thresholds** – Weight loss, tumor volume, necrosis area, and clinical signs must have **numeric thresholds** tied directly to euthanasia or additional treatment.

## 4. Animal Numbers, Sex, and Model Choice
- **Weak justification of N** – “Standard in the field” alone is not enough. Committees look for **statistical reasoning** (power, α, variance) or strong precedent, plus realistic attrition assumptions.
- **Sex selection not justified** – Single‑sex designs must be backed by **data and literature** (e.g. more homogeneous tumors in females). Otherwise, there is a risk of needing a second, male‑only study, increasing total animal use.
- **Unrealistic ages and reserves** – Very old animals in large numbers (e.g. many 24–30‑month‑old mice) or large “reserve” percentages (e.g. 15% extra animals) are questioned unless well justified and logistically realistic.

## 5. Procedural Detail and Scientific Coherence
- **Missing key parameters** – Typical gaps include: virus type and immunogenicity, dose and route of hypertrophic/oncologic agents, exact sound intensity/frequency, or frequency/number of imaging and intravital procedures per animal.
- **Incoherent or duplicated group definitions** – Repeated group labels, mis‑labelled controls, or text that does not match tables undermine confidence that the protocol is actually executable as written.
- **Risk of uninterpretable data** – When expression of a construct or success of a model cannot be verified (no tagging, no plan to confirm expression), large animal use can lead to negative but **uninformative** results, which is an ethical problem.

## 6. Documentation Quality and Self‑Containment
- **Reliance on attachments for critical information** – Pain scoring tables and key endpoint logic appearing only in annexes, not summarized in the main form, are frequent reasons for “not approved yet.”
- **Copy‑paste and language mismatches** – Inconsistent terminology (music vs sound stimuli, genetically modified vs wild‑type, female vs “female and female”) and partially updated text (old doses left in tables) signal low document quality.

---

**Practical takeaway:**  
Protocols are rejected less for their scientific ideas and more for **how clearly and specifically they translate those ideas into animal‑level welfare safeguards**: staged design, complete analgesia/anesthesia plans, sharp humane endpoints, realistic and justified N/ages/sexes, and internally coherent documentation that does not rely on guesswork by the committee.
