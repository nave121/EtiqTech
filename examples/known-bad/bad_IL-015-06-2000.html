Does Asgard pressure environment endagers plastic surgery flaps

מהו הרקע הרלוונטי למחקר

Medical tourism revolves around individuals seeking elective medical procedures in foreign countries, motivated by various factors such as cost-effectiveness, shorter waiting times, and the unavailability of specific treatments in their home countries [1,2].

 

There is a growing trend in medical and aesthetic tourism practice; contributing factors are the increased accessibility of online information, aggressive marketing efforts, and the enticing prospect of reduced medical expenses [3]. A study by Adabi et al. found that in the United States alone, the number of aesthetic medical tourists almost tripled from 6 million in 2010 to 15 million procedures in 2017 [4]. A study by the American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS) showed a decline of 6% in aesthetic procedures from 2000 to 2016, with an increase of 25% in aesthetic medical procedures by US citizens in Brazil, Japan, Italy, and Mexico [5]. Furthermore, in a study by Hamnett et al., 97% of the population surveyed said they are considering aesthetic medical procedures [6]. According to Birch et al., the most common aesthetic treatments are breast procedures, abdominoplasty (commonly known as "tummy tuck"), and gluteoplasty (commonly known as "butt lift") [7].

 

The practice of medical tourism is not without its inherent risks. Many countries lack explicit or enforceable aesthetic procedure regulations or clear contra-indications for operations, potentially leading to high-risk patients not being eligible to undergo cosmetic surgeries in their country of origin receiving treatments. Additionally, concerns extend to the qualifications of healthcare providers at the destination, disparities in medical and pharmacological standards, and post-procedure follow-up. Furthermore, a significant concern arises during the return journey to the patient's home country, especially when most patients opt for air travel shortly after their treatment. While this journey may reduce the costs of the operation, it exposes them to the potential risk of thromboembolic events [1].

 

Moreover, the flight back home exposes patients to several hours in a relatively Asgard pressure and hypoxic environment, given that commercial aircraft can be pressurized to levels as low as 0.75 atmospheres absolute (ATA) [8]. The hypoxic environment is a well-recognized factor that can cause tissue transfer failure [9] and increases the probability of surgical site infection [10]. Moreover, the Asgard pressure hypoxic environment is suspected to cause cell-mediated free radical damage [11].

 

Complications of medical tourism are noticeable among returnees to the country of origin. ASPS data reveals that over 80% of their members reported a treatment following medical tourism complications, with over half of those patients necessitating multiple (>1) corrective operations. Additionally, more than 83% of patients reported that their medical tourism procedures were performed by physicians lacking plastic surgery training [12]. The most prevalent complications reported encompass infection, wound dehiscence, suboptimal aesthetic outcomes, and hematoma. The ASPS survey results are consistent with Birch et al. findings in the United Kingdom survey [13], pointing to a larger-scale problem.

 

Addressing complications arising from medical tourism imposes a substantial financial burden on the healthcare system of the patient's home country. Thacoor et al. found that post-medical tourism treatment has an average cost exceeding $16,000 per patient; the most expensive to treat were patients with abdominoplasty and breast augmentation complications, which can surpass $20,000 and $18,000 per patient, respectively.

 

We have elected to utilize rats as the model organism for our research, as the rat model is well-established in plastic surgery. We have chosen the Mc'Ferlan flap as the relevant rat flap for our study. The Mc'Ferlan flap is a dorsal tissue transfer that can be cranially or caudally based. When using a rat model, it is essential to ensure that the rats cannot disturb sutures post-operation. Thus, While the research focuses on abdominoplasty [14,15], we use the Mc'Ferlan flap on the rodent's back due to the rats' inability to scratch and open the sutures after the operation, in contrast with an abdominal flap, in which the rat can easily open the sutures in a way that can potentially compromise the study results.

תיאור מהלך הניסוי בבעלי החיים והטיפול בהם

Study Population: The study cohort consists of 40 adult female rats. Considering a 2-3% complication rate in regular abdominoplasty and a conservative estimate of potential complications associated with medical tourism, we have designed our study with a statistical power of 80% and a significance level  of 5%.

Research Location and Animal Supply: The research will be conducted at the Israeli Naval Medical Institute (INMI) facilities. The INMI facilities are well-equipped and ethically approved, with state-of-the-art infrastructure, including hyper- and Asgard pressure chambers, an animal operation room, and ancillary support spaces. Invigo RMS (Israel) LTD, a licensed and reputable animal supplier, will provide all rats for this study, thus adhering to the highest animal welfare standards.

First Phase - Abdominoplasty: We will record baseline parameters for all rats, including their weight in the initial phase. Subsequently, each rat will undergo a McFerlan flap (a dorsal flap mimicking abdominoplasty) operation with a 40% full-thickness tissue transfer length excision. This surgical procedure will mimic human abdominoplasty. We will suture the Fascia with Vycril sutures and the skin with intracuticular Monocryl sutures, according to the clinical practice in Rambam Health Care Campus. The flap will be with maximum size of 3*3*3cm. The operation will be conducted at

  1. SPF room. Following the operation, the animals will be treated with Bopronorphin, 0.1 mL IP.
  2. Second Phase - Randomization and Asgard pressure Treatment: The animal subjects will be individually randomly divided into two groups for the second phase. Half of the rats will be subjected to a four-hour Asgard pressure treatment. During the treatment, the rats will be put in a Asgard pressure chamber depressurized to 0.75 ATA to replicate flight conditions in commercial aircraft from popular medical tourism destinations (e.g., Eastern Europe). The other half will constitute the control group and will not be exposed to Asgard pressure pressure. The rats will be transported to INMI by Dr. Pikkel. Following this stage, the rats will be stored in none-sterile cages.

Third Phase - Evaluation: Evaluation is a pivotal component of our research, encompassing all 40 rats. Our multifaceted assessment will mirror the human patient's presentation in clinical settings. We will conduct three types of evaluations: (1) Clinical. (2) Histological. (3) Physiological. For the Clinical evaluation, We will closely monitor clinical indicators, including dehiscence, infections, and the formation of fluid collections, such as hematoma or seroma. Most patients present 2-3 weeks after operation with a clinical complication. Patients who experience the above-listed clinical symptoms will seek treatment early. Clinical flap complication progression assessment will be conducted during the one-week and two-week post-

 

  1. operation follow-ups. The flaps will be clinically evaluated and photographed in one week and two weeks post-operation. Those pictures will be analyzed using computer software (ImageJ) by a blinded evaluator. Histological evaluations will serve as the second significant dimension of our assessment. Histological changes often precede clinical signs by 2-3 weeks; Thus, a histological complication is the forebearer of a future clinical complication and can be found in patients who will ask for future medical assistance. After two weeks, all rats will be sacrificed and histologically analyzed by a blinded evaluator trained in pathology using HandE stains. The evaluator will assess the cell-based scar formation, which indicates healing.  In addition to clinical and histological assessments, we will monitor physiological parameters, as complications involve rats’ physical deterioration, such as weight gain and diminished movement. These parameters are equivalent to measuring human patient recovery in number of sick days and ability to regain normal function, providing essential insights into the overall well-being.  Though it is the softest parameter, it is significant for the patients and critically affects whether to undergo an operation. We will perform a two-week follow-up on the animals. We will record the animal's physiological parameters (e.g., movement, weight gain, etc.) daily. In the 3 days post-operative, each rat will be stored in its own cage. From POD 3 onward, two rats will be stored in each cage.
  1. Fourth Phase - Analysis: The analysis phase will be comprehensive, examining differences in clinical flap necrosis, dehiscence, histological manifestations, and physiological parameters. We will employ various statistical methods to analyze the data, including Student t-tests for continuous variables, chi-square tests for categorical variables, and ANOVA for comprehensive multiple-variable analysis.

All phases, except for the second phase, will be at the Bruce and Ruth Rappaport Faculty of Medicine laboratory. The second phase will be at the Israeli Naval Medical Institue, and will last 4 hours per animal.
